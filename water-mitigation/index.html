Water is still spreading: what to do now

Water mitigation is the emergency work that stops water damage from growing: shutting off the source, starting extraction, mapping moisture and setting drying equipment. If you see water or damp materials now, call the 24/7 emergency line at (562) 271-5850 while you take the first steps. Same-day response is available when crews are free, and no arrival time is promised.

Shut off the main water valve if a pipe or appliance is leaking. Cut power to wet rooms only if the panel is dry and reachable, and keep people and pets out of dark or sewage-smelling water. Lift furniture legs onto foil, pick up rugs and take photos.

My carpet squishes and the wall is damp: is that mitigation?

Yes. A squishy carpet, a wall that feels cool or damp, and baseboards that are starting to swell all mean water is still inside your house. The trade term for the response is water mitigation, and its goal is to stop the damage from spreading before repairs begin.

Water you can see on a floor is usually a fraction of what has moved into the pad, the drywall and the framing behind it.

Structural drying

Structural drying is how a wet building gets back to normal. Air movers push air across wet surfaces, and LGR dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the air so it does not settle back into materials. In wall cavities, injectidry or cavity drying forces warm, dry air through small holes so the studs and insulation dry without opening the whole wall.

Moisture meters check drywall, wood and subfloor, and thermal imaging cameras show cool wet spots behind finishes. Crews record readings in a drying log, and the goal is for wet areas to match readings in dry reference materials from an unaffected part of the building.

Monitoring visits are typical, and equipment is adjusted or removed when readings show progress. No honest company can promise a number of days up front, since materials, water category and humidity all play a part. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard governs this work, and you can ask any company about it.

When water in the house is an emergency

The U.S. EPA says water-damaged areas and items should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth. In practice that means mitigation cannot wait for the next business day. Night and weekend calls are routine, and the line is answered whenever you call.

Treat a loss as an emergency if the source is still running, water has reached a second room, a ceiling is sagging, or the water is not clean. If water touches electrical outlets or a gas appliance, call the utility or 911 first.

What happens in the first 24 to 72 hours

The sequence is the same on most jobs, though each loss differs.

  • 1
    Stop the source. The valve is shut off or the leak is isolated so no more water enters.
  • 2
    Extract. Pumps, extraction units and wet vacuums remove liquid water. See emergency water removal for that step.
  • 3
    Map the moisture. Meters and thermal imaging show where water traveled, including behind walls and under floors.
  • 4
    Set the equipment. Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and wall-cavity drying are placed and logged.
  • 5
    Monitor and adjust. Crews return to read the meters and move or remove equipment as readings fall.

Mitigation versus repair, and why leaks come back as mold

Mitigation dries the structure, and water damage repair rebuilds drywall, flooring and cabinets afterward. Skipping the drying step is how a wall ends up hiding wet insulation behind fresh paint.

Mold needs moisture, and the EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control. If wet materials are not dried, or the leak is not fixed, growth becomes a separate job, and you can read about mold remediation for that. Mitigation is not waterproofing, either. It dries what got wet and does not make a building resist future water.
